# Earth Radio

Earth Radio is an interactive globe for listening to NASA Earth visualizations. Choose one of 12 curated places, press **Play station**, and hear a sequence of three short tracks (36 total). Every track combines that station's NASA visual layers into one synthesized arrangement driven by rendered pixels. The pins are entry points into historical imagery, not sensors or live broadcasts.

## Run locally

From this directory, start a static server:

```sh
python3 -m http.server 8000
```

Open <http://localhost:8000/> in a modern browser. No build step, account, API key, or external network is required after the included assets are present. Sound starts after pressing Play. Drag the globe or use its keyboard controls; the searchable station list reaches the same places. The optional **Guide** explains the three main controls. Each station has three tracks (roughly one minute each) that advance and wrap automatically. Previous and Next move through the station's tracks; pause and resume preserve position in the current track. The song-position slider scrubs the generated composition without changing any NASA source video timeline. The globe displays the currently selected NASA flat-map source as its principal texture, before and during playback. The small view icons select that station's sources, and **Next view** cycles through them. The plain-Earth icon hides the map overlay while leaving NASA visual inputs in the sound. A retractable source window sits above the view icons and shows the original NASA frame, including moving-camera films that cannot be placed accurately on a map. Collapse it to a narrow title bar; it starts collapsed on smaller screens unless you have saved another preference. The player's **Sound & sources** control opens the full source viewer. Source cards there let you inspect another video or still, change its image focus, or scrub **that source's** video. Volume starts low; the app pauses when the tab is hidden.

The first Play at each station in a session starts a short synthetic host introduction. It plays once for that station, not on every track change or resume. **Skip intro** and **Mute host** are in the receiver; after the clip ends, **Replay intro** is available. Host mute is saved locally in this browser. The intro script is readable in **How this sound is made**. The music briefly fades down under speech and returns afterward. The main volume slider also limits host level; setting it to zero silences both.

Use the local HTTP server rather than opening `index.html` as a `file://` URL; direct file access can block canvas analysis. The app needs no external API or account. Run the focused audio regression with `node tests/playlist.cjs`.

## What the sound represents

`audio.js` loads the station's sources on separate, muted media elements. It samples small rendered frames in a canvas and maps simple image properties to Web Audio oscillators, filters, pulses, and gain. Station layer weights mix the resulting voices. Three arrangements change the relative layer levels, pitch register, pulse rate, note pattern, and texture without restarting the visual timelines. A steady eight-step beat grid schedules synthesized low drum, soft noise hit, high ticks, station motif, night glints, and forest plucks on one Web Audio clock. Flat ocean frame changes shape shared beat accents; historical lightning-map brightness can add quiet, grid-timed ticks. Moving GEDI and CO₂ camera views do not drive the shared groove. GEDI frame motion instead gates limited woody plucks, while CO₂ colour shapes its global bass. The aerosol still affects an air texture but has no temporal motion. Visual brightness and colour shape other voices, and the grid's base tempo remains stable. Three cinematic ocean excerpts and one atmospheric-river excerpt are visual-only context; they add no voice. Only ready sound layers contribute. The music is generated locally in Web Audio; no NASA or environmental audio is sampled. Track progress measures time actually heard after a source frame is ready, not a NASA dataset timestamp. The focused visual and its controls are managed by `app.js`; `globe.js` renders the globe and its station pins. `index.html` and `style.css` provide the interface. Media is bundled under `assets/` so canvas sampling works without cross-origin restrictions.

Each station has a hand-tuned rhythm profile that changes low-drum pitch, high-tick tone and density, and the level of a soft noise hit. Ocean frame change can add shared accents. California uses softer hits, the Gulf Stream deeper ones, the Western Atlantic / Erin and Kuroshio / Japan denser ticks, the Mediterranean brighter ticks, Etna / Ionian coast a restrained beat, Sahara sparse hits, Agulhas broad low hits, and Hawai‘i more space. Aerosol colour also shapes a synthetic air texture. These are musical interpretations of the selected imagery, not claims about sounds, weather, or musical traditions at those places. The About and source details explain this in the interface.

Each station also has a hand-composed motif and three tempo/register variations, so its tracks retain a recognizable musical shape. Motifs and tempos are artistic choices; NASA pixels shape the layered voices and some percussion accents in real time. Bay of Bengal, the Indian Ocean south of Java, and Brazilian Atlantic use offshore ocean and aerosol focus areas checked against the bundled maps. The Bay of Bengal and Brazilian Atlantic stations also use separate night-light focus areas toward nearby coasts. These are broad editorial regions from independent historical visualizations, not exact or simultaneous observations.

The sampled focus window spans about 9 of 96 analysis pixels by 9 of 54, which is roughly 34° of longitude by 30° of latitude on a global flat map. It is a broad visual sample, not a point reading. Track progress starts when a source becomes ready and pauses when playback is paused or the tab is hidden; a temporary media stall may still advance the displayed clock.

## Globe layers

Selecting a station turns and zooms the globe toward its editorial marker; manual drag, pinch, wheel, keyboard arrows, and zoom buttons remain available. The view-icon strip selects a NASA globe view; its plain-Earth icon hides the globe overlay without changing the music. People who prefer reduced motion get an immediate camera change and no beat ring or animated overlay refresh. The searchable station list offers the same selection without using the globe.

The globe shows **one selected NASA flat-map visualization at a time**, over the credited Blue Marble base. Explicit equirectangular assets include [Perpetual Ocean 2](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5505/), [Black Marble annual night maps](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5475/), [ISS lightning history](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5011/), and the single-pollutant [PM2.5 model](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5518/). The older [ocean animation](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/3912/), [EIC night-light left panel](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5313/), and [aerosol still](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5669/) are draped approximately. The globe renderer assumes each flat map spans 180° W to 180° E and 90° N to 90° S. Coastlines were visually compared with Blue Marble, but the excerpts have no pixel-level registration check. The retractable NASA source window carries the original source labels and timeline; the four repeated year stamps are masked **only on the globe's Black Marble texture**. Source videos keep their own historical timelines. Changing the focused view does not change the multi-source composition.

When night lights are the selected view, the globe draws a spatially distributed subset of warm, bright pixels sampled from its actual annual-map frame. Those positions are approximate because the NASA panel is a presentation graphic. Their glow and the visual window's blue bar follow the **synthesized kick beat**: the beat's timing is a composed grid and its accent is shaped by the active visual frames, including the selected night-light focus. The pulsing does **not** represent a physical change in light output or a synchronized observation. Reduced-motion preference keeps the sampled lights static. The plain-Earth icon turns the map view and beat cue off, but the source frame remains viewable. The full visual and mappings remain available under **Sound & sources**.

The globe applies a **display-only** colour treatment to make visual patterns legible. Flat-map layers are blended more strongly over Blue Marble; EIC and Black Marble night lights receive a dark-threshold brightness lift, while the historical lightning layer receives a smaller lift. This preserves the source pixels’ locations but changes their displayed intensity and can compress the brightest highlights. The original NASA media remains visible in the source window and is used unchanged for the sound analysis; the boosted globe is not a calibrated light, pollution, or event measurement.

The globe caches its rendered texture when only the beat cue changes, limits overlay sampling to roughly 4 frames per second (about 2.4 on devices reporting fewer resources or narrow screens), and uses a smaller globe texture on those screens. At rest it renders near the display's physical pixel size, capped at 1024 pixels on capable desktops and 640 on lean screens; camera movement temporarily reduces that work. Source frames are sampled into a map up to 1536 × 768 on capable desktops, limited by the source's useful width; lean devices use a 640 × 320 map. The source preview updates at most twice per second. Visual-only camera films preload metadata and decode only while selected; changing views pauses them. This reduces duplicate canvas and video work; playback on low-power physical devices still needs profiling.

A faint ring at the selected marker follows synthesized kick beats using the Web Audio clock. It is an interface cue for the music, not a NASA event or a local observation. Camera-moving GEDI, CO₂, ocean close-up, and atmospheric-river frames stay in the stage window because their pixels cannot be assigned to fixed globe coordinates. The historical ISS lightning map **is** projected, but its 10-day moving window is not a live strike feed; musical ticks are quantized interpretations of brightness, not individual events. The aerosol still can be projected approximately but never animates by itself.

## Interpretation limits

- The sound is an artistic mapping of **rendered image pixels**, not a recording of Earth or a calibrated reading of temperature, current speed, light output, canopy height, aerosol concentration, or CO₂ parts per million. Video compression, labels, color scales, and camera movement can affect it.
- Stations use approximate areas of global maps. The GEDI camera moves over a regional scene; the CO₂ camera orbits Earth. Their selected pixels do not track a fixed geographic coordinate.
- The ocean, night-light, GEDI, CO₂, and aerosol sources cover different periods. A composition juxtaposes independent historical timelines; it does not reconstruct simultaneous conditions. The aerosol source is one still frame. The video slider controls only the selected source, and elapsed playback time is not a calendar date.
- The night-light excerpt ends before the source video switches to its later change and zoom views. NASA's annual-map frames display their own year labels (2012–2023), but Earth Radio does not parse or separately display those labels. Holds and fades are uneven, so elapsed video seconds cannot be converted into a year by a simple formula. The poster is a frame from within the included excerpt.

The globe uses WebGL for full-resolution rotation when available, with an optimized Canvas 2D fallback.
